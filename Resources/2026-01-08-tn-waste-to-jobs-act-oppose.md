This guide explains the Tennessee Waste to Jobs Act (Senate Bill 0269, or SB0269) using clear language. You'll learn what the bill does, how it works, and—especially—what risks might arise if it becomes law. Information draws from the official bill text, fiscal analysis, and discussions about similar laws. The bill was introduced to the 114th Tennessee General Assembly in early 2025 and aims to tackle packaging waste through a system called **Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)**. Supporters tout its potential to boost recycling and create jobs, but critics warn about increased costs and added complexity.

---

## What Is the Bill in Simple Terms?

Imagine you buy cereal in a box or takeout in a plastic container. Packaging like this frequently goes to landfills because Tennessee's recycling programs are underfunded and inconsistent—only about **12%** of packaging is recycled, with nearly **900,000 tons** going to landfills yearly.

The Waste to Jobs Act proposes shifting responsibility for this waste away from taxpayers and local governments to the companies that make or sell the products (called "producers").

**In essence:**

- **Producers** (food companies, manufacturers, importers, etc.) must join a nonprofit group called a **Producer Responsibility Organization (PRO)**.
- The **PRO** collects fees from producers and uses these funds for statewide recycling, reuse, and composting programs.
- **Goals:** Reduce waste, create thousands of new recycling jobs, and develop better recycling infrastructure—all without raising taxes.
- **Exemptions:** Small businesses, governments, certain nonprofits, and select items (like medical packaging or long-lasting containers) are exempt.
- The bill amends Tennessee's solid waste laws (**Title 68, Chapter 211**) and sets key deadlines:
    - Producers must join/form a PRO by mid-2026
    - Full compliance starts in 2030
    - Penalties for non-compliance: up to **$100,000 per day** for repeat violations

---

## Key Parts of the Bill

### Who Pays and Why?

- Producers pay **"dues"** to the PRO, based on the amount/type of packaging they introduce in Tennessee.
    - Lower fees for eco-friendly packages (reusable, recyclable, or made with recycled content)
    - Higher fees for hard-to-recycle materials
- The PRO uses these funds to:
    - Reimburse local governments, schools, and nonprofits for recycling costs
    - Invest in sorting/recycling facilities
    - Educate the public
    - Build markets for recycled materials

### The PRO's Role

- The PRO is a nonprofit, approved by the Tennessee Department of Environment and Conservation (**TDEC**).
- Every few years it creates a 5-year plan that:
    - Assesses needs (e.g., where recycling is lacking)
    - Sets performance goals (like increasing recycled packaging content over 5, 10, and 15 years)
    - Prioritizes reducing packaging, promoting reuse, and minimizing recycling contamination
    - Handles overall operations

### Requirements and Oversight

- Producers can’t sell packaged goods in Tennessee after 2030 without PRO participation.
- **TDEC** oversees the program, aided by an **advisory board** (with representatives from producers, local governments, and environmental groups).
- Annual reports will track progress, spending, and material flows.
- Large counties (population over 200,000) must participate; smaller ones can opt in (to receive reimbursements).

### Goals Stated in the Bill

- **Create jobs** and fund recycling **sustainably**
- **Divert packaging** from landfills and recover valuable materials
- Build a **circular economy**—reusing materials instead of wasting them
- **Fiscal note:** State costs are estimated at $1.1-1.2 million annually (mainly for staffing/oversight), covered by PRO fees. No direct tax increases, but indirect effects are possible.

## Potential Risks If the Bill Passes

While the bill aims to solve waste problems, critics point out significant concerns based on experiences with EPR laws in California, Colorado, Maine, Minnesota, and Oregon.

### 1. Higher Costs for Businesses and Consumers

- Producers may pass new PRO fees on to consumers, raising prices for everyday goods.
    - In other states, "eco-modulated" fees have driven up product costs by 1–5% or more, depending on the material.
    - Small to medium businesses (those over $2 million in revenue or handling over 1 ton of packaging) might feel the squeeze.
- The Flexible Packaging Association opposed earlier bill versions because of **"flawed producer definitions,"** which could unfairly burden some firms and increase operational expenses.

### 2. Job Losses or Shifts in Certain Sectors

- While recycling jobs might increase, industries like distribution, manufacturing, or waste management could lose jobs if fees make Tennessee less competitive.
- Previous "bottle bill" proposals faced pushback for threatening jobs in specific sectors; similar effects could occur if producers relocate or reduce operations.

### 3. Administrative Burdens and Compliance Challenges

- Businesses must:
    - Track packaging types and quantities
    - File new reports
    - Join the PRO
= all of which means more paperwork and costs.
- With no national EPR standard, companies operating in multiple states will have to navigate a "patchwork" of conflicting rules, complicating supply chains and increasing the risk of legal/bureaucratic headaches.
- The PRO could act as a monopoly with little competition, potentially creating inefficiencies or favoritism in fee/practice setting.

### 4. Ineffective Waste Reduction or Unintended Environmental Issues

- EPR laws sometimes fail to cut non-recyclable packaging production due to being "fragmented and uncoordinated."
- If participation is low or infrastructure weak, waste may continue to pile up—raising costs without real results.
- More contamination in recycling streams is possible if educational efforts fall short.
- The bill allows exporting collected materials, which can simply shift pollution elsewhere rather than truly closing the resource loop.

### 5. Legal and Interstate Commerce Risks

- Some say state-level EPR may violate the U.S. Commerce Clause by burdening out-of-state companies, inviting lawsuits.
- Tennessee's bill targets goods "entering the state," making it possible for national producers to challenge it.
- Conflicts with differing targets and rules in other states could cause confusion, as seen in the patchwork of EPR laws nationwide.

### 6. Impacts on Local Governments and Taxpayers

- While reimbursements sound helpful, mandatory participation for large counties could strain local resources if PRO funds fall short.
- The fiscal note describes local impacts as **"indeterminate,"** so it's unknown whether PRO fees will cover all costs.
- Smaller communities opting out might have fewer recycling options, increasing inequities.

---

## **Summary**

If passed, the Waste to Jobs Act would mark a major change in Tennessee's approach to packaging waste. While it could create new jobs and improve recycling, risks include economic pressure on businesses and consumers, logistical and legal challenges, and the possibility of failing to meet its environmental goals.

**If you oppose the bill, consider contacting your legislators or partnering with advocacy groups. Stakeholder feedback has already resulted in changes to previous versions.**
